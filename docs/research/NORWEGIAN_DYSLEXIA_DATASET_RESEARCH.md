# Norwegian dyslexia dataset research

**Research date:** 8 September 2026  
**Purpose:** identify authentic Norwegian spelling errors that can be used to evaluate a local, open-source contextual spell checker, with particular emphasis on children and teenagers with dyslexia.

---

## Executive summary

The central finding is that **Norway has abundant authentic pupil-writing data, but very little openly downloadable writing data that is explicitly linked to a formal dyslexia diagnosis**.

This matters because the two things are not interchangeable. A general pupil corpus can tell us how Norwegian children actually misspell words, but it cannot honestly be described as a dyslexia corpus. Likewise, the weakest 5% of spellers are highly relevant to the product problem, but they are not necessarily dyslexic.

The strongest sources found fall into three groups:

1. **Confirmed dyslexia, researcher-held data.** The best leads are the Bergen Longitudinal Dyslexia Study and the Dysmate-C validation dataset. Both contain actual written responses from children known to have dyslexia, but raw error strings are not publicly released.
2. **Large authentic Norwegian pupil corpora.** The NORM Corpus and DigiHand provide real pupil writing with original spellings preserved and corrected versions available. These are extremely useful for benchmarking and model development, but are not dyslexia-labelled.
3. **Poor-speller / low-performing groups.** Skaathun/Eidevik material and similar studies analyse the weakest spellers in large Norwegian samples. These are useful for understanding severe spelling-error patterns but should not be labelled as dyslexic unless diagnosis is independently known.

A realistic strategy is therefore:

- immediately build a large **general Norwegian spelling-error benchmark** from NORM and, where licensing allows, DigiHand;
- separately pursue access to **diagnosis-linked spelling responses** from Dysmate-C and the Bergen longitudinal study;
- keep second-language learners separate from first-language Norwegian writers;
- do not use synthetic errors as the primary benchmark, but use them later for stress testing;
- aim for a layered benchmark rather than one undifferentiated dataset.

The most promising route to **1,000+ diagnosis-linked error/correction pairs** is Dysmate-C. Its validation study included 50 children already diagnosed with dyslexia in the word-spelling analysis, and the spelling task contained 40 contextualised target words. That creates up to 2,000 response opportunities before accounting for early stopping. The key unknown is whether the researchers retained the actual typed response strings in an accessible form.

## 1. Dataset categories

For Skrivi, every example should carry a provenance category.

### A. Confirmed dyslexic writing

Writing or spelling responses from people with a formal dyslexia diagnosis, or from a research cohort explicitly classified as dyslexic using stated criteria.

This is the highest-value material for the final benchmark.

### B. General Norwegian pupil writing

Authentic school writing by Norwegian children without a dyslexia label. This is still extremely valuable because it captures real child spelling errors, compounds, dialect influence, punctuation, word-boundary errors and developmental spelling patterns.

It should be used for broad calibration and comparison, not presented as dyslexic data.

### C. Second-language errors

Norwegian written by pupils whose first language is not Norwegian. These errors can overlap with dyslexic spelling but arise from different mechanisms. They should be separable in the benchmark.

### D. Synthetic errors

Errors deliberately generated by researchers, rule systems or language models. These are useful for edge cases and regression testing, but should not substitute for authentic human writing.

## 3. NORM Corpus

### Why it matters

NORM is the best immediately identifiable source of **large-scale authentic Norwegian child spelling errors**.

The corpus contains around **5,200 texts** from Norwegian primary-school pupils, collected at **24 schools** across Norway. Pupils are approximately **8–13 years old**. The material includes urban and rural schools, Bokmål and Nynorsk schools, pupils with Norwegian as a first language and minority-language pupils using Norwegian as a second language.

Each pupil text exists in three forms:

1. scanned original;
2. transcription preserving what the pupil actually wrote;
3. corrected / normalised transcription.

The original and corrected transcriptions are linked.

### Particularly useful feature

The NORM documentation gives an explicit corpus query for finding **all places where the pupil's original word differs from the corrected form**:

```text
a:[] :: a.word != a.orig
```

Search results can be downloaded as Excel, tab-separated text or CSV.

That means NORM can potentially produce thousands of real Norwegian error/correction pairs without requiring manual re-transcription.

### Language and population controls

The corpus includes both Bokmål and Nynorsk and lets researchers select subsets. It also includes metadata that can help distinguish first-language Norwegian pupils from minority-language pupils. This is important because L2 errors should not be mixed into the dyslexia benchmark without a label.

### Dyslexia status

**Not identified.** NORM must not be described as a dyslexia corpus.

### Access and licensing

Språkbanken lists the resource under **CLARIN_ACA-NC-LOC-ND**, with rights described as academic / CLARIN and restrictions including non-commercial and no-derivatives conditions.

Practical implication:

- excellent for research and internal evaluation if the licence terms and access conditions are satisfied;
- **do not assume we can redistribute extracted error/correction pairs in the public Skrivi repository**;
- ask the Text Laboratory / rights holder for explicit permission before publishing a derived benchmark.

### Priority

**Very high.** Use it to build the first large authentic-Norwegian benchmark, with provenance labelled `general_pupil` rather than `dyslexia`.

Sources:

- https://www.nb.no/sprakbanken/en/resource-catalogue/oai-tekstlab-uio-no-norm/
- https://tekstlab.uio.no/brukerveiledninger/norm/
- Resource interface: https://tekstlab.uio.no/glossa3/norm

## 4. Bergen Longitudinal Dyslexia Study

### Why it matters

This is one of the clearest Norwegian sources of **actual writing from children with confirmed dyslexia**.

Frøydis Morken and Turid Helland published *Writing in Dyslexia: Product and Process* in 2013 using data from the Bergen Longitudinal Dyslexia Study.

The study compared:

- **13 children with dyslexia**;
- **28 children without dyslexia**;
- all approximately **11 years old**.

Children completed a sentence-dictation task on a computer. **Five sentences** were read aloud twice, and the children typed them as accurately as possible with no time limit. Keystroke logging recorded the writing process.

The data were analysed for:

- spelling errors;
- grammar errors;
- semantic errors;
- transcription fluency;
- revisions.

### What may still exist

To produce those analyses, the research team necessarily had the typed outputs and keystroke logs. These are not publicly released in the material found during this research.

Even if only the final five typed sentences per child remain accessible, the dyslexic group gives **65 diagnosis-linked sentence responses**, likely containing a meaningful number of word-level errors.

### Dyslexia status

**Yes. Confirmed study group.**

### Access and redistribution

No public raw-data licence was found. Access would require asking the research team / UiB data steward whether anonymised response-level data can be shared for non-commercial research and whether derived word pairs could be published.

Because the participants were children and the study predates current open-data norms, consent limitations may prevent public redistribution even if internal research access is possible.

### Priority

**Very high as a contact lead**, but probably too small to reach 1,000 pairs by itself.

Sources:

- Paper: https://doi.org/10.1002/dys.1455
- Project: https://www.uib.no/en/project/speakup
- Project contacts: https://www.uib.no/en/project/speakup/62082/contact-information

## 5. Dysmate-C validation data

### Why it matters

This is probably the strongest single route toward a **large diagnosis-linked Norwegian spelling dataset**.

Trude Nergård-Nilssen and Oddgeir Friborg developed and validated Dysmate-C using data from **more than 1,100 Norwegian pupils**.

The spelling task consisted of **40 common Norwegian words** of increasing length and complexity. It deliberately included orthographically difficult forms such as silent letters and homophones. Importantly, each target word was presented **inside a sentence so its intended meaning was clear**, then repeated. The child typed the target word on a computer with spelling and grammar checkers disabled.

The spelling test was used for grades **3–7**.

In the published discrimination table, the word-spelling sample contains:

- **449 typical children**;
- **50 children with a formal dyslexia diagnosis**.

The test stopped after five consecutive spelling errors, so not every child necessarily attempted all 40 words.

### Potential scale

For the 50 diagnosed children alone, there are up to:

**50 × 40 = 2,000 spelling-response opportunities**

before early stopping is taken into account.

If the database retains the exact typed response string rather than only correct/incorrect item scores, this one source may be sufficient to produce the majority of a 1,000-pair diagnosis-linked benchmark.

### Critical unknown

The paper publishes test scores and psychometric analyses, not the raw spellings. We need to ask:

1. Were the exact typed spellings retained in the research dataset?
2. Can anonymised response strings from the formally diagnosed subgroup be shared?
3. Can target words and response pairs be used for an open-source research benchmark, or are the target items protected assessment material?
4. If target items cannot be released, could the researchers release **error-pattern statistics** or a de-identified set of naturally occurring misspelling/correction pairs stripped of test ordering and scoring information?

### Language

The validation study is Norwegian. Current Dysmate-C materials are available in Bokmål and Nynorsk, although the precise language distribution of the original validation spelling responses should be confirmed with the authors.

### Priority

**Highest-priority contact.**

Sources:

- Full article: https://journals.sagepub.com/doi/10.1177/15345084211063533
- DOI: https://doi.org/10.1177/15345084211063533
- Dysmate research information: https://www.dysmate.no/mer-om-testene/
- Current Dysmate-C description: https://www.dysmate.no/tavla/ny-dysleksitest-for-barn/5/

Primary researcher:

- **Prof. Trude Nergård-Nilssen**, UiT. The article lists `trude.nergard.nilssen@uit.no`.

## 6. DigiHand

### Why it matters

DigiHand provides unusually useful open research material from **beginning Norwegian writers**, particularly for Nynorsk.

One major DigiHand study followed:

- **90 first graders** learning to compose primarily by handwriting;
- **91 first graders** learning to compose digitally;
- **181 pupils total**;
- five writing occasions over roughly eight months.

The participating schools were in Western Norway and used **Nynorsk as the main written standard**.

### Original and corrected text

The study's transcription process is highly relevant to Skrivi.

Researchers first produced a raw character-by-character transcription preserving spelling. They then parsed text into words and created another version **corrected for spelling**. The published appendix gives real examples such as:

- `fårtlinge` → `fortellinga`
- `basenge` → `bassenget`
- `kåm` → `kom`
- `håbe` → `hoppe`
- `sbist` → `spist`

The researchers also explicitly handled **real-word contextual errors**. Eivor Finset Spilling's thesis notes that a form can be a valid Norwegian word in isolation but still be wrong in the sentence, exactly the kind of problem a contextual spell checker needs to solve.

### Availability

The published study states that data and analysis material are available on OSF:

- https://osf.io/j7ne3/

The paper and coding documentation explain the transformations from raw text to spelling-corrected text.

### Dyslexia status

**No.** These are beginning writers, not a dyslexia-labelled cohort.

### Licensing

The journal article is CC BY 4.0. The OSF dataset is publicly accessible, but the licence attached to the actual dataset components must be checked directly before redistributing pupil-derived text in Skrivi. Public accessibility alone is not sufficient evidence of permission to republish the dataset.

### Priority

**High**, especially for Nynorsk and for testing contextual real-word errors. Use as a `general_pupil` benchmark.

Sources:

- Study: https://www.sciencedirect.com/science/article/pii/S0360131523000325
- Data: https://osf.io/j7ne3/
- DigiHand protocol DOI: https://doi.org/10.23865/njlr.v6.2115
- Eivor Finset Spilling thesis: https://uis.brage.unit.no/uis-xmlui/bitstream/handle/11250/3061844/Thesis_Eivor%20F%20Spilling_open.pdf

## 7. Skaathun / Eidevik poor-speller material

### Why it matters

A 2014 master's study by Eidevik analysed data from the norming of **Staveprøven** by Skaathun.

A later UiS thesis describes the underlying Bokmål norming dataset as containing **3,444 pupil responses from grades 3–10**. Eidevik focused on the **lowest-performing 5% of spellers** and analysed their spelling errors and spelling mastery.

This is potentially very valuable because it covers school ages close to Skrivi's intended users and deliberately isolates severe spellers.

### Dyslexia status

**Not established.** The lowest 5% of spellers cannot automatically be treated as dyslexic.

### Access

The raw response-level data were not found as a public download in this research pass. Access is likely to require tracing the Staveprøven research/test owners and the Eidevik work through Norwegian university repositories.

### Legal concern

Assessment test items can be protected because public release can invalidate the test. Even where researchers are willing to share anonymised error strings, they may not be able to release the original target words or full item set publicly.

### Priority

**Medium-high contact lead** for authentic severe spelling errors, with strict provenance label `poor_speller`, not `dyslexia`.

Source confirming the sample and Eidevik analysis:

- https://uis.brage.unit.no/uis-xmlui/bitstream/handle/11250/299216/Aven_Lene.pdf

## 8. LingDys / Everway as a possible historical error source

LingDys is not an open dataset, but it is relevant because its spell-checking system has been designed specifically for dyslexic Norwegian writing for more than 20 years.

Public teaching material demonstrates that the system is intentionally expected to handle severe phonological misspellings such as:

- `sjino` → `kino`
- `jæj`
- `gåteri`

and the example sentence `jei vill på sjino`.

LingDys currently supports both Bokmål and Nynorsk.

### Why contact them

A long-lived dyslexia spell checker may possess one or more of the following internally:

- historical misspelling lexicons;
- hand-built replacement rules;
- anonymised error logs;
- evaluation sets;
- phonological-error dictionaries;
- research material inherited from earlier NTNU/Lingit work.

No evidence was found that these resources are publicly downloadable.

### What to ask

Ask whether Everway/Lingit would be willing to release or license a **non-commercial, anonymised evaluation-only subset** of historical Norwegian dyslexic spelling pairs, perhaps without frequencies or proprietary ranking information.

### Priority

**Medium.** Potentially high value, but commercial and uncertain.

Sources:

- https://lingit.no/produkter/
- https://lingdyspluss.no/velkommen/gjor-oppgaver-med-marianne/
- Example educational material mentioning `Sjino -> kino`: https://sprakloyper.uis.no/sites/default/files/2021-06/Skrivetrappa-1.pdf

## 10. Second-language and synthetic errors

### Second-language Norwegian

NORM explicitly includes minority-language pupils using Norwegian as a second language. Those examples are valuable, but they should be marked separately.

Recommended benchmark label:

```text
writer_group = norwegian_l2
```

Do not silently mix them into a first-language dyslexia set.

### Synthetic errors

Synthetic examples can be useful for deliberately testing:

- single-character insertions/deletions/substitutions;
- phonetic spellings;
- compounds;
- doubled consonants;
- `og/å`;
- real-word contextual confusions;
- dialect-influenced forms;
- proper nouns;
- unusual but legitimate sentences.

However, synthetic data should primarily be used for **coverage and regression testing**, not as evidence that the product works for dyslexic writers.

Recommended label:

```text
writer_group = synthetic
```

## 11. Recommended benchmark structure

Rather than one dataset called "Norwegian dyslexia errors", build a layered benchmark with provenance for every item.

Suggested fields:

```text
id
source
source_record_id
writer_group
formal_dyslexia_diagnosis
age_or_grade
language_standard
norwegian_first_language
original_sentence
original_token
corrected_token
corrected_sentence
error_type
contextual_real_word_error
collection_method
licence
redistributable
notes
```

Suggested top-level groups:

### Gold: diagnosed dyslexia

Only formally diagnosis-linked responses.

Target: **1,000+ error/correction pairs**.

### Silver: severe / poor spellers

Authentic errors from the lowest-performing spelling groups without confirmed diagnosis.

### Broad pupil benchmark

NORM and DigiHand examples from ordinary pupils.

Target: several thousand pairs across ages, Bokmål and Nynorsk.

### L2 benchmark

Kept separate from first-language pupils.

### Synthetic stress set

Small and deliberately designed, not used for headline accuracy claims.

## 12. Best route to 1,000+ high-quality dyslexic pairs

### Step 1: contact Dysmate / Trude Nergård-Nilssen first

This is the best numerical opportunity.

Ask specifically whether the validation database contains **the literal strings typed by the 50 formally diagnosed children** on the 40-word spelling task.

If yes, request:

- anonymised typed response;
- intended target word;
- grade/age band;
- Bokmål/Nynorsk if available;
- no participant identifiers;
- permission for internal non-commercial evaluation;
- separate permission, if possible, to release derived error/correction pairs publicly.

Even if public redistribution is impossible, a private evaluation set would be extremely valuable.

### Step 2: contact Frøydis Morken / Bergen Longitudinal Dyslexia Study

Request the final typed sentence outputs or a derived word-error table from the 13 diagnosed 11-year-olds.

This source is smaller, but it is valuable because it contains **sentence context**, unlike isolated spelling tests.

### Step 3: build the large general-pupil benchmark now

Use NORM to extract authentic original/corrected word differences, while respecting access and licence restrictions.

Keep:

- Bokmål and Nynorsk labels;
- age/grade where available;
- L1/L2 distinction;
- surrounding sentence context.

This gives us enough data to evaluate whether the local model understands real Norwegian child spelling even before diagnosis-linked data arrives.

### Step 4: use DigiHand for Nynorsk and contextual edge cases

DigiHand's raw and corrected text transformations are particularly valuable for beginner spelling and contextually wrong real words.

### Step 5: ask for severe-speller material

Trace the Skaathun/Eidevik material and ask whether anonymised error/correction pairs can be shared without exposing protected test content.

### Step 6: if necessary, collect a small consented Skrivi corpus

If researcher-held data cannot be released, recruit through Dysleksi Norge, specialist teachers and willing schools.

A very small protocol could generate a surprisingly useful benchmark:

- 50 writers with documented dyslexia;
- 20 short sentence prompts or short free-writing tasks each;
- original text preserved exactly;
- intended correction confirmed by the writer or a qualified adult;
- explicit consent for anonymised research use;
- separate consent for public release if desired.

The goal should be **error pairs with context**, not collecting large personal essays.

This minimizes privacy risk and makes annotation manageable.

## Bottom line

There is enough authentic Norwegian pupil-writing data to build a serious benchmark immediately, but **the diagnosis-linked dyslexia subset is the scarce resource**.

The highest-value next action is not more web scraping. It is a targeted data-access request to the researchers behind **Dysmate-C** and the **Bergen Longitudinal Dyslexia Study**.

If Dysmate retains the literal typed responses from its 50 formally diagnosed children, that dataset alone could plausibly move Skrivi close to the goal of 1,000 high-quality diagnosis-linked spelling-error/correction pairs. NORM and DigiHand can then provide the much larger comparison set needed to determine whether the spell checker works broadly without overfitting to severe spelling errors.
